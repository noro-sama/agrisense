Plain-language guides for farming with Zambia's rains — not against them.

El Niño and La Niña, simply

El Niño happens when Pacific Ocean waters warm up. For Zambia it usually means the rains start late, stop and start, and give less than normal. The 2023/24 season — the drought many farmers remember — was an El Niño season.

La Niña is the opposite: cooler Pacific waters, and Zambia often gets more rain than normal. Good for rain-fed fields, but risky for waterlogging and washed-out roads.

The label matters less than the forecast. Not every El Niño brings drought to every district — check the current outlook for your area before deciding, e.g. with our plant check.

Managing dry spells

A dry spell of two to three weeks inside the season can hurt more than a late start, because crops are already growing. What helps:

  • Mulch early. Cover the soil between rows with dry grass, leaves, or leftover stalks before the soil bakes hard. Mulch holds moisture and cools the roots.
  • Weed on time. Weeds drink the same water your maize needs. A weedy field feels a dry spell first.
  • Water what you can. Even small irrigation — watering cans, a treadle pump, or bucket drip lines on a portion of the field — can carry seedlings through a gap in the rains.
  • Plant with the first good rains. Crops planted early develop deeper roots before mid-season dry spells arrive.

Fertiliser timing: follow the seed packet

Many seed brands print exact fertiliser timing — for example, basal at planting and top dressing about 4 weeks after the crop emerges. Keep that schedule. Applying late because you are waiting for rain can stunt growth and waste the fertiliser.

If a dry spell is forecast around fertiliser time, do not shift the window — protect the moisture instead:

  • Mulch before applying, so the soil holds water around the roots.
  • Irrigate if you can, so granules dissolve and reach the roots.
  • Never heap concentrated fertiliser onto bone-dry soil with no water coming — it can burn the roots.

Rule of thumb: the calendar decides when to fertilise; the sky decides how you protect the crop around it.
